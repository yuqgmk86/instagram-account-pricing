## Followers come in tiers, and the tiers are priced per listing

Marketplaces that carry audience-bearing accounts split them into bands rather than fixed SKUs: 1K, 5K, 10K, 50K, and 100K+ followers. Price moves with the band, but also with engagement rate, niche, audience country, and whether the seller can show story views and comment quality.

What to check before paying for any tier:

- **Account age** — creation date or posting history, not just follower count
- **Engagement** — likes, comments, and story views against follower count
- **Niche** — a fitness audience won't convert for a SaaS product
- **Audience country** — matters if you sell locally or run geo-targeted ads
- **Delivery speed** — instant on some listings, days on others

An account with 10K engaged followers in a buying niche outperforms 50K bought followers every time. Sellers who can't show engagement numbers are telling you something.

## Account age does not buy you safety

Sellers push aged accounts hard, and their argument has a real basis: new registrations hit rate limits fast, get asked for phone or ID verification after trivial activity, and often can't run ads at all for the first weeks. An account with a genuine posting history and a consistent login pattern clears those thresholds more easily.

That argument covers platform risk only. It says nothing about whether the account is worth owning.

A twelve-year-old account earns $33 a month. An eight-year-old cat page has 12.8K followers and no revenue attached. Age and value are unrelated numbers.

So separate the two questions. Age answers "will this account get action-blocked on day one." Content history, niche, audience quality, and monetization answer "is this account worth what they're asking."

One more thing worth knowing before you buy an audience: followers drift. Marketplace guidance is explicit that follower counts "may change over time" depending on engagement, niche, and platform activity. If you buy 50K and lose 3K in the first month, that's normal churn, not fraud.

## The warranty clause most buyers void within an hour

Both major sellers in this market offer replacement guarantees if the account fails on arrival. The gap between "covered" and "not covered" sits in a single sentence buried in the setup instructions.

One seller's terms state it plainly: contact support **before** you change the login email, password, or recovery details. Changes made without notifying them void the replacement warranty.

Read that again against the standard advice everyone gives. The normal instinct after receiving credentials is to immediately change the password, then the email, then the phone number, all within ten minutes. Do that and the warranty is gone before you've posted anything.

The order that keeps coverage intact: message support first, tell them you're about to secure the account, then proceed.

What else falls outside warranty coverage, per published seller terms:

- **Follow/unfollow bots and automation apps** — named as the main cause of action blocks, and excluded

- **Changing username, email, and phone on the same day** — the account gets flagged and the seller treats it as buyer error

None of the listings reviewed states a refund window in days. "Replacement if the account fails on arrival" is the whole promise. Ask what "on arrival" means in hours before you pay, because that's the only deadline that matters.

## Your first 72 hours with a new account

This sequence combines the setup guidance published by two different sellers. Neither covers all of it alone.

1. **Contact support before touching credentials** if your seller's warranty requires it. One message now protects the replacement.
2. **Log in from one device on one IP.** Keep it there. Logging into multiple accounts from the same device is a named risk factor.
3. **Change the password first, then the email, then the phone number** — spread across separate days, not one sitting.
4. **Turn on two-factor authentication** using an authenticator app rather than SMS.
5. **Update the profile in stages.** Photo on day one, bio on day two, username later in the week. Never all at once.
6. **Behave like a person.** A few likes, a couple of comments, one post a day for the first week. No mass following, no bulk unfollowing, no automation of any kind.
7. **Wait before switching to a Professional account.** A week or two of normal activity first, then convert for insights.

Bulk account operators need a different setup: separate proxies, separate devices or browser profiles, and no cross-login between accounts. Mixing ten accounts on one IP is the fastest way to lose all ten.

## When buying is the wrong move

Three cases where the money is better spent elsewhere.

**You need a specific handle.** If your brand name is taken, buying an account with a different username doesn't solve it. You'd be paying for someone else's history under someone else's name.

**You want the blue check.** Verification isn't reliably purchasable. Marketplace listings that mention verified accounts qualify it as "subject to seller details," which means availability depends on the individual seller and isn't guaranteed by the platform.

**You're building a founder-led brand.** An audience attached to a previous owner's voice doesn't transfer. If the content depends on you being you, buying 20K followers who followed someone else gets you a number and no engagement. Starting from zero costs less than buying the wrong audience.

Buying makes sense when the account is infrastructure: a second profile for a client, an ad-ready asset for a media buyer, a niche page you plan to run with your own content from day one.
